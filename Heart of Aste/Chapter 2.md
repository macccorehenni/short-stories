# Chapter 2
I woke up lighter. Not just rested—unclenched. Something inside me had let go in the night, leaving behind an odd sort of silence under my ribs. The bed under me was far too soft, the kind that conspires with your body to keep you still. Dawn stretched herself across the curtains; the light that slipped through came in crooked, hesitant lines that warmed everything it touched.
And Flicker… he hadn’t moved one inch.
I felt him before I saw him—a small blot of warmth balanced atop my head. He was still tangled in my hair, breathing those tiny, even breaths that made the strands lift with every exhale. His mane had gone dim while he slept, only a faint glow pulsing through the braid. Every time he shifted, static lifted the shorter hairs along my scalp.
The problem, of course, was the nest: he’d lodged himself right against my braid again. Maybe it smelled like me, maybe it simply kept its shape better than anything else, maybe he liked the sound of my pulse under there. Whatever the reason, it wasn’t stable. When the braid loosened—which it always did—he reacted like gravity was plotting against him.
I shifted to sit up, and sure enough the braid slipped about half an inch. Flicker startled awake with a wild brrrp! and vanished in a snap of light. For a fraction of a second I thought he’d disappeared completely. Then he flickered back into place, fluffed to twice his size.
I lost it—burst out laughing right there.
He answered with another indignant chirp, like the braid itself had personally offended him, then scrambled deeper into my hair until only a faint warmth betrayed where he’d gone.
“Morning to you too,” I murmured, rubbing gently at the spot. He stilled under my fingers, his glow smoothing out until it matched the rhythm of my heartbeat.
---
Across the room, Korrin groaned into her pillow. “Please tell me that creature is still not living on your head.”
“Afraid so,” I said.
She peeled one eyelid open, hair a complete disaster, and smiled. “Figures. He’s claimed you. Hope you like commitment.”
Flicker chirped once, proud and unapologetic, his little body pressing closer. And I—well, I couldn’t even pretend to mind. His trust sat warm against my neck, heavier than it looked.
---
Later, the silver man waited for us in the east hall, perfectly motionless. He always looked more carved than alive. Light slid across him too evenly, refusing to decide whether he was metal or flesh.
“Are you sure about this?” I asked. My arms had folded before I noticed. The air around us felt charged—like even the castle was listening.
He didn’t blink. “We must go farther,” he said. Each word fell smooth and polished, like a sound repeated too many times until it lost its edge.
Flicker stirred uneasily in my hair, claws tightening a strand. I ran a finger over his back to settle him, though the same tremor ran through me too.
Something old inside me—the same thing that once guided me through Callendor—rose again, steady as the tide. Not faith exactly. Not courage either. Just that slow, bone-deep knowing that the path was already waiting for me to follow it.
I exhaled. “Then we go.” The words didn’t ring heroic. They just… happened.
He inclined his head the tiniest bit, already turning to lead. Flicker huffed once in protest but stayed tucked in place, his warmth anchoring me.
---
It was supposed to be day, but the Fields refused to cooperate. Light hung low and thin, as if it regretted showing up at all. The ground rolled outward in endless shadowed patches, cut by strange lanterns that blinked on and off like faulty stars.
Korrin squinted ahead. “This is day? Really?”
“The Fields don’t care what we call it,” I said.
“Well, they could at least meet us halfway.”
Another lantern winked out. Flicker’s mane rose instantly, throwing his own small light forward. I could feel him trembling against my neck, so I reached up and pressed my fingers to that faint glow.
“Hey. Still here,” I whispered.
He softened at the sound, pulse steadying after a few seconds.
Korrin glanced over and smirked. “Braver than I am.”
“He’s small,” I said. “Doesn’t know when he’s supposed to be afraid.”
That earned me a sharp, scandalized chirp. Korrin’s laugh echoed faintly in the half‑dark, the first normal sound I’d heard all day.
---
By the time we stopped, every muscle in my legs hated me. Korrin was muttering something about “eternal twilight torture,” and even the silver man paused—not that he’d ever admit fatigue.
I dropped onto a hollow stone ledge and made the mistake of brushing dust from my shirt. Flicker saw the movement as an opportunity.
He sprang from my shoulder and landed squarely on my chest, eyes bright with purpose.
Before I could ask what that purpose was, he pounced on the single crumb clinging to my shirtfront and gulped it down whole.
“Flicker,” I groaned. “That was lint.”
He chirped, clearly pleased, licking one tiny fang with unearned pride.
Korrin wheezed out a laugh. “He’s going to eat your clothes next.”
Flicker puffed his mane twice as big. Offended.  
Then, just as I opened my mouth to scold him, he let out the smallest burp—a silvery puff rising from his mouth like smoke from a half‑dying candle.
Korrin nearly choked laughing.
I sighed and rubbed my forehead. “One day, you’ll set my hair on fire.”
“Could be an improvement,” Kira muttered from behind us without looking up from her gear.
Flicker squeaked angrily from the safety of my braid. I patted him once, pretending to smooth the strands. “Ignore her,” I whispered. “You’re perfect. Just flammable.”
---
He’ll stay there for hours, I thought. But then the floor beneath us shifted—no, shivered. The vibration crawled up my boots before the roar even reached us.
That sound wasn’t something you heard so much as something that reached straight through you, deep and hungry. Korrin froze mid‑step. Kira’s conjured flame snapped smaller, jittery. Even the silver man’s head turned slightly, as if listening to a language older than ours.
And then the demon stepped into view.
The sound hit first, then the shape—too many shadows moving at once. The thing dragged itself forward out of the hall, claws scraping the stone until sparks jumped. It was like the last demon but wrong in heavier ways: broader, a jaw too tight with teeth like freshly cut glass, its eyes burning red enough to make the corridor breathe heat.
Flicker moved before I did.  
One fierce brrrp! and he was a streak of silver light, darting toward it.
Kira followed on instinct, fire flaring up her arms; the air filled with that hot‑iron smell that always came before she lost her restraint. Korrin slammed both palms to the floor, releasing another sickening shockwave straight down the hall.
The demon still came.
I had time to take half a breath, maybe curse under it, before instinct—or something beneath instinct—took the wheel. A surge of power rose fast and unfamiliar. Suddenly my hands knew weight: a hammer, heavy and cold, balanced like it belonged there.
There wasn’t room to think. I threw.
It screamed forward, caught the light, and hit the monster’s spine with a noise that cracked the air. A gem, dark and pulsing, tore loose from the creature’s back and fell, rolling toward us, leaving trails like dying fire behind it.
The demon staggered, clutching for something that was no longer there. Shadows peeled away from it in sheets.
Flicker struck again—a bright curve of movement—his body almost doubled in size now, mane fanned out like a small explosion. He was still tiny for what he was trying to fight, but the force behind him landed anyway.
Korrin’s shockwave hit, followed by the roar of Kira’s fire catching the upper half of the demon’s body.
When it finally collapsed, it was smoke, ash, and the smell of burnt stone. The hall went still except for our breathing—harsh and uneven and far too alive.
I blinked hard, staring at where my hammer should have been.  
Gone.
The gem dimmed on the ground, pulsing a heartbeat or two longer before fading.  
And the silver man—he was gone too.
Not ahead. Not behind. Just disappeared like he’d been erased.
Korrin slumped back into the wall, the color barely holding in her face. Kira knelt, her flames guttering low as she checked her sister.
Flicker half‑fell, half‑glided to the stone beside them, small again and shaking, his light trembling with exhaustion. I stooped to catch him. He pressed himself against my palm but still stared toward the space the silver man had left, a faint questioning sound in his throat.
Kira was the first to speak. “He didn’t help.”
“He didn’t stay,” I said, the words so quiet they nearly dissolved.
Flicker made a sound that might have been agreement before he went limp against my hand.
---
The castle didn’t speak for a long time after that.  
Not a whisper, not a hum, not even the creak of settling stone.
Flicker slept through entire days. Curled somewhere near my braid, so faint I had to keep checking for warmth.  
Korrin kept finding excuses to visit—half to read aloud, half to make sure he was still there.
“He saved me,” she said once, pressing her fingers against his tiny paw. “He’s earned every hour of this nap.”
I nodded. Couldn’t bring myself to say much else.
One night, when the hallway clocks had given up keeping time, something changed. A small hiccup of silver slipped from my hair, faded midair, then came the faintest chirp—barely more than a breath.
Korrin froze midsentence. Then she smiled so wide it hurt. “There he is.”
He didn’t crawl out. Just twitched once, pressed closer to my skin, tried, maybe, to hide his own weakness.
“He burned through himself for us,” I murmured.
“For me,” Korrin corrected softly. Her thumb brushed along my braid as if trying to thank him through it.
Flicker’s faint trill vibrated once more, pride wrapped in exhaustion.

---

Days drifted, but not all of them were gray.  
We started moving again—not away, just within. Reclaiming pieces of space the castle had forgotten.
Korrin stacked her hoard of books near the old fireplace, muttering that proximity to dust counted as scholarship. Kira took over the courtyard—said she needed a safe distance for her fires, but we all knew she just liked the view.

I reopened the windows. They resisted at first, their hinges cramped from years of disuse, but they yielded with a groaning breath that spilled sunlight and wind that smelled faintly of iron and moss.
Flicker followed us, still sluggish at first. Sometimes he perched on my shoulder, silent sentinel mode—head cocked one way, tail twitching slow. Other times he dozed in my collar and let me carry his peace for both of us.
He started to recover in those odd little ways: warming a patch of stone that never seemed to hold heat, chasing floating dust motes, trilling if anyone—especially Korrin—got too close to my hair.

---

We found the flower on the third day. That’s what Korrin called it anyway—a flower.  
I almost missed it; it had forced itself between two pieces of broken stone like it was stubbornly refusing to die there. Its petals glowed faintly, same color as Flicker’s dusk light, maybe softer.
We cleared the space around it, our hands raw from prying loose rock. Then we carried it to the courtyard and set it in actual dirt, sunlight sliding shyly through the haze above us.
Flicker insisted on helping. He pawed at the soil, half disappearing when he got tired only to flicker back a few inches away like he’d cheated and teleported. By the time we finished, he was panting faintly, but proud—so proud.
He pressed his tiny crown against the flower’s stem and released one strict pulse of light.
The petals brightened.
Something humored the stone beneath us—light seeped through the cracks, faint silver lines trailing out through the courtyard, then farther, threading their way up pillars and over the archways. The castle warmed under it. It wasn’t sudden. Just steady.
Korrin’s breath caught. “He did that,” she whispered, as though saying it too loud might undo it.
Flicker puffed his mane and squinted at the flower, satisfied, before shuffling tiredly back to my braid.
He made one sleepy sound—half a chirp—and went limp, his warmth steady against my skin again.
I brushed his back lightly. “Sleep, little storm,” I told him.
The air around us felt different after that—warmer, more forgiving, like the castle itself had unclenched.
And for the first time, I almost believed this strange, broken place wanted us here.

---

"I feel like we should go hunt for the silver man." Korrin frowned, Flicker finding a new home in her short hair. 
"Why? If he wanted to be  here, he would be."
Mira's right cheek flared a warning. Her smudge responded whenever she felt threatened, and it was the brightest it had been. 
"He's nothing but a deadbeat," Kira continued, anger in her voice. "He _abandoned_ us!"
"You do realize the boat man turned out to be a relative of aunt Yaereene? What if silver man is important?" I retorted. 
Flicker's small, bright, ember-like eyes flicked towards Kira and me from our little sister's hair. He was concerned, ember streaks flowing through his ash grey fur. I caught sight of his eyes and he retreated to the roots of her medium green hair. 
"Everything's alright, dusk-cub."
He frowned, something I hadn't seen before. I don't think he agreed with me. 
"We'll go farther today, but not to find silver man."
