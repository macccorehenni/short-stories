# Chapter 4— The Forest Gives

## Irin

Running. Always running.



Yaereene didn’t say it out loud, but I felt it in her field the moment we stepped into the street — that tight, braced‑for‑impact feeling she gets when she’s already planning three exits ahead. I hated that feeling. Hated how it made my skin buzz, how it made the world tilt like it was waiting to pounce.



The innkeeper wouldn’t look at us when we left. That was the first sign.  

The second was the way Yaereene’s hand brushed my arm — not grabbing, not guiding, just… checking. Making sure I was still there.



I pretended not to notice.



The city was waking up around us, all green stone and forest‑breath, but it felt too quiet. Too watchful. Like the trees themselves were holding their breath.



I kicked a pebble down the road, trying to shake the tension out of my shoulders.



“We’re not staying,” I said, even though she hadn’t asked.



Yaereene nodded once.  

That was all.



Fine. If the world wanted to try us again, it could try.  

I was done being afraid of shadows.



I squared my shoulders, lifted my chin, and started walking.



If we had to run, then we’d run on my terms this time.

Yae had noticed the deliberate slow pace I had set, and kneeled to my height. 

“This hasn’t been fun for me either, sister, but weakness is _not_ an option. Either we continue moving, or…” Yae’s voice thinned, the rest swallowed by pain she didn’t bother to hide.



I felt the words hit me like a shove. Not cruel — just true in that way only she could make truth feel. I kicked at a loose stone, pretending it didn’t land.



“I’m not being weak,” I muttered, sharper than I meant. “I’m just tired of running without knowing why.”



The admission scraped out of me before I could stop it. I hated how small it sounded. Hated that she’d hear the fear tucked under the frustration.



I straightened my shoulders, forcing my voice steady.



“But fine. We keep moving. Just… don’t talk like I’m the one slowing us down.”



“I do understand your pain. I’m feeling it too. But we keep moving, okay?” Yae said, her voice steadier than it had any right to be. I could see the strain in her eyes, the way she shoved her own hurt aside to make room for mine — like protecting me was the only thing keeping her upright.



I nodded, swallowing the tears that had gathered before I even realized they were there. My throat burned, but I forced the feeling down, locking it behind my ribs where it couldn’t slow us.



We kept walking.



"Halt."



The dreaded voice cut through the street — cold, metallic, belonging to someone who had long since stopped caring whether people lived or died.



Yaereene didn’t hesitate. She grabbed my wrist and bolted toward the trees, her breath sharp, her field spiking with fear she didn’t have time to hide. I stumbled after her, but the moment we crossed the first line of roots, something inside me snapped.



Not broke.  

Snapped open.



A feeling surged up my spine, violent and overwhelming, as if my whole body had decided to buzz like a million bees trapped beneath my skin. My vision blurred at the edges. My fingertips tingled. The air around me thickened, humming, vibrating, alive.



I knew instantly what it meant.



A red‑shirt.  

Close. Too close.



And my grief — the grief I had been swallowing for days, the grief I had refused to name — tore free all at once. It didn’t feel like crying. It felt like burning. Like something inside me had been waiting for the exact wrong moment to ignite.



A crackle leapt across my knuckles.  

A spark jumped from my wrist to the nearest tree root.  

The air tasted like metal and storm.



I gasped, clutching my hands to my chest, but the buzzing only grew louder, brighter, angrier.



This wasn’t fear.  

This wasn’t adrenaline.



This was power — wild, electric, and nothing like the magic I had seen in stories. It was grief made unbearable, grief made dangerous, grief made real.



I had never felt anything like it.  

I never should have.



But it was mine now.

## Yaereene

“What was _that_?” I hissed, the words slipping out sharper than I intended.



The air around Irin still crackled, faint threads of light skittering across her skin before fading into the forest’s shadow. I stepped toward her, keeping my voice low, controlled, though my pulse hammered against my ribs.



“That wasn’t fear,” I said. “And it wasn’t chance.”



I reached for her wrist, felt the tremor beneath her skin — not panic, not exhaustion, but something alive, something waking. The scent of ozone clung to her like a second field.



“Irin… that was power.”



She stared at me, eyes wide, breath shaking. For a heartbeat she looked younger than she had in years — not because she was afraid, but because she didn’t understand what her own body had just done.



“What was _that_?” she whispered again, this time to herself, as if hoping the forest might answer for me.



I held her gaze, steadying my own fear before it could reach her.



“Something new,” I said quietly. “Something dangerous. And something we don’t have the luxury of ignoring.”



Her hands sparked once more — a tiny, involuntary flicker — and she flinched.



I didn’t.



“Come,” I murmured, guiding her deeper into the trees. “We keep moving. Whatever this is… we face it together.”



As I thought about it, the shape of the moment finally settled. 

What had happened was obvious in hindsight. 

Just as I had, only days before, she had crossed that invisible threshold. 

She had found her magic.



I recognized the look in her eyes—the disorientation, the way her breath caught as if her own body had surprised her. 

It was the same fracture-point I’d stumbled through, the same quiet rupture in the world’s logic. 

And for a heartbeat, I wasn’t alarmed. 

I was… relieved. 

Because I knew exactly what she was feeling.

## Irin

“My magic?” I said it before I could stop myself, and it came out all pouty and ridiculous. 

I don’t have magic. I’m not half‑elf like my sister… I don’t have magic. 

That thing that happened earlier — that was a fluke. A weird accident. 

“What magic?”



And then I realized I was getting flustered at myself, which only made it worse. 

My hands were already doing that fluttery thing, sparks nipping at my fingertips like they were trying to argue with me.



“I mean— I didn’t do anything on purpose,” I muttered, heat crawling up my neck. “It just… happened. Once. That doesn’t count.”



I could hear my own heartbeat in my ears, loud and traitorous, like it knew something I didn’t.



I could hear my own heartbeat in my ears, loud and traitorous, like it knew something I didn’t.



“You may be human, Irin, but… how else would you explain what happened to the Lich?” Yae said, flicking a pebble with a little too much precision. 

It skipped farther than it should have. 

She was getting better at her magic.



I swallowed hard. “I—I don’t know. Adrenaline? Luck? He slipped?”



Yae gave me that look — the one that felt like she was peeling back layers I didn’t even know I had. Calm. Certain. Annoyingly unshakeable.



“Lightning doesn’t come from luck,” she said.



And my fingertips tingled again, sparks gathering like they were waiting for me to stop lying to myself.



“You’re jealous,” Yaereene said, as if she were noting the weather. 

The observation did not help my mood. 

The sparks grew, crackling at my fingertips like they had opinions.



“If this really is magic — which it isn’t — yours is much cooler,” I snapped, trying to smother the heat in my face with logic that wasn’t helping.



“So you’re jealous. Understood.”



She said it so simply, so infuriatingly matter‑of‑fact, that I almost tripped over my own breath. 

I wasn’t jealous. I was… overwhelmed. Confused. Possibly malfunctioning. 

But jealous? Absolutely not.



Except the sparks didn’t agree with me, and Yae’s raised eyebrow said she’d noticed.

“Hmm, which way should we go?” Yaereene mused, as if my rising frustration were a breeze she could simply walk through. 

She tugged me forward, her attention already drifting to the treeline.



We’d made great progress in a single day — the trees had grown thicker and taller, their canopies knitting together overhead. 

The distraction had worked. 

She’d kept me talking, kept me thinking about anything except the sparks still prickling at my fingertips.



I glanced up. The sun was all but gone, swallowed by branches that hadn’t been this dense a few hours ago. 

A shiver crawled up my spine.



“Yae,” I said, trying to keep my voice steady, “it’s getting dark.”



She only hummed in acknowledgment, eyes scanning the shadows like she could read them.

"Perhaps we should find refuge then."

The field looked harmless enough at first glance — just an open stretch of grass that went gold at the tips when the wind brushed through. But the longer we stood there, the more wrong it felt.



The air had a shimmer to it, like heat rising off stone even though the night was cool. Little threads of light drifted low to the ground, curling around our boots before slipping back into the grass. Not bright enough to be alarming, just… present. Watching. Waiting.



Every time the breeze shifted, the magic shifted with it — a soft swirl, almost like the field was exhaling. The grass bent in patterns that didn’t match the wind, spiraling in slow, deliberate arcs that made my skin prickle.



We camped in the middle of it — much to my consternation — and the moment I set my pack down, the ground hummed under my palms. Not loud. Not dangerous. Just a reminder that this place was alive in a way fields shouldn’t be.



Yae seemed perfectly at ease.



I, very much, was not.



“Yaereene!” Her voice cracked on my name, high and bright with panic.



Something gnawed at me. The field was glowing — faint at first, then pulsing, brighter each time we breathed. Irin stumbled back, sparks snapping off her fingertips in frantic little bursts.



“Yae, it’s— it’s getting stronger,” she said, words tumbling over each other. “Why is it doing that? Why is it doing that?”

“Yaereene!” It ripped out of me before I could think. Too loud. Too sharp. My throat hurt with it.



The field was glowing. It wasn’t supposed to glow. It was just a field — dead grass, cold air — but it was lighting up like someone had struck a match under the dirt. And it kept getting brighter. Every breath I took made it worse. I could feel it. I swear I could feel it.



“Yae, it’s— it’s doing something,” I said, but the words tripped over each other, all tangled. Sparks jumped off my fingers without me calling them, little frantic snaps that made the glow pulse harder. “It’s reacting to me, I don’t know why, I’m not doing anything—”



The light surged. My magic spiked. I felt it yank at me, like a hook behind my ribs.



“Stop— stop— stop,” I hissed, shaking my hands out like that would help, like I could fling the feeling off. It didn’t stop. It pulled harder.



Yae reached out with her power — I felt the shift in the air, the way it always goes still around her, like she’s smoothing the world flat. For a second the glow faltered as Yaereene threw a stone—



Then it shoved back. Hard. I felt the hit in my teeth.



“Yae, it’s pulling on me,” I said, voice cracking again. “I’m not feeding it, I’m not, it’s taking it, it’s taking it from me—”



The ground hummed. The light climbed up my legs like heat.



And then — gods — I felt it reach for her too.



She moved before I could even breathe. Verdant Green flashed in her hand — I didn’t even see her draw it — and she carved a clean arc through empty air. Something unseen shrieked with laughter, a bright, chiming sound, and fled so fast the grass bent in its wake.



“Sister, are you alright?” Yae asked, already lowering the blade as if she hadn’t just swung at a ghost.



“I’m— I’m alright,” she added with a soft laugh, brushing a stray leaf from her sleeve. “We must be getting close to the High Forest. I should have been more careful there.”



“Who was *that*?” My voice came out breathless. I could feel my pulse in my teeth. My hands were still sparking from the panic.



“It must have been a seelie,” Yae said, as if naming a bird she’d seen on a morning walk. “Pay no mind to them. Just brandish your power.”



Easy for her to say. My power was still jittering under my skin, trying to decide whether to fight or flee.



“Yae, it *touched* me,” I said, swallowing hard. “It laughed.”



“They do that,” she replied, stepping ahead as if the path itself parted for her. “Come. The High Forest is near. They won’t bother us if you look like you can bite back.”



I tried to steady my breathing. Tried to look like someone who could bite back.



But the truth was: I could still feel the echo of that laughter brushing the inside of my ribs. It was a weird emotion— scary, yet angry at the same time. 

But then Yae flicked a pebble at me with her telekinesis — a sharp little tap to the shoulder — and despite everything, despite the glow and the seelie and the panic still buzzing in my bones, I laughed. It burst out of me before I could stop it.



I let my power answer her. Just a small arc, a playful snap of energy toward her ribs.



Yae yelped — actually yelped — and stopped walking, hand pressed to her side. For a heartbeat I panicked, thinking I’d hurt her, really hurt her.



Then she looked at me.



“You just controlled your power,” she said, breath catching, but not from pain. From pride. Real pride. It washed over her so fast it softened every line of her face.



And then she smiled — truly smiled — the way she used to before her mother fell. Not the tight, polite curve she’d been wearing for weeks. Not the brave mask she kept putting on for me.



A real smile. Warm. Unguarded. Mine.



It hit me harder than the pebble.

“Halt!”



I’d heard that word enough times to flinch before I even thought about it. But this wasn’t fire snapping at our heels or a red‑shirt barking orders. The sound came from the trees themselves — soft, rooted, watching. A Forest guard.



Yaereene stepped forward like she’d rehearsed this moment her whole life. I stayed beside her, muscles locked, pretending my stillness was a choice.



“I am Yaereene Inphyra, Queen of the Feydor Forest,” she said, voice steady as stone. “I am being hunted as a prisoner of war and request refuge — for myself, and for my sister.”



Sister. The word hit me harder than the guard’s stare.



He looked us over like he was reading a map only he could see.

“Your race… curious. Half‑elf, yes, but something deeper.”



Yaereene didn’t move. So I didn’t either. Not because I was calm — because if I moved, I’d run.



“You carry Fey blood,” he said. “Both of you. But there’s more. Something layered. Something… interesting.”



My throat tightened.

“What do you mean?”



“Prophecy.”



The guard said it like it was a taste he recognized — something old, something he’d been waiting to name. His smile wasn’t cruel, but it wasn’t comforting either. It felt like the forest leaning closer.



“You may pass to find it,” he continued. “Have you anything else?”



Yaereene inhaled, slow and deliberate, the way she does when she’s trying to keep her crown from slipping — the invisible one she still insists on wearing even while running for her life. Her shoulders stayed high, regal, unshaken. She was already thinking three steps ahead, already imagining what “prophecy” meant for her people, her forest, her duty.



I wasn’t thinking anything that noble.



My stomach dropped. Prophecy meant danger. Prophecy meant being seen too clearly. Prophecy meant someone else had already decided what we were supposed to become.



I felt the word settle on my skin like cold ash.



Yaereene answered first, of course. “We seek only safety and truth.”



Her voice didn’t waver. Mine would have.



The guard’s eyes flicked to me — not unkind, but curious in a way that made my pulse jump. Like he could see the part of me I didn’t have a name for yet. The part that wasn’t elf, wasn’t Fey, wasn’t anything I understood.



I swallowed hard.

“No,” I said, though it came out quieter than I meant. “Nothing else.”



But the guard kept looking at me, as if he didn’t believe that for a second.



Yaereene stepped half a pace closer to me — protective, subtle, the way she always is when she senses I’m about to bolt. Her hand didn’t touch mine, but I felt the intention of it like a tether.



The forest shifted around us, branches creaking in a language I didn’t know but somehow felt in my bones.



Prophecy.



The word wouldn’t leave.

## Ferris

I was tracking the red root then — the one I didn’t yet know was Feybloom. I’d followed its thread through the underbrush, thinking only of the plant, its shape, its scent, the way it curled like it was listening.



That’s when I saw them.



Her first: the half‑elf with the long, dark green hair that didn’t belong to any High Forest bloodline I knew. It moved like a living thing, like the canopy answering the wind. I didn’t understand it then. I only knew I couldn’t look away.



And beside her, the sister — light blue twin tails catching the sun like riverwater. Both of them out of place in a forest of blond and brown heads, mine included. They looked like they’d stepped out of a story older than the trees themselves.



I’d come for a root.

Instead, I found the two people who would change the shape of my life.

Later that day, in my tannery, the bells I had attached to the door rang. It was them. 

"Hello, are you the ones who came today?"

Yaereene bowed, her long body all but folded in half. 

"Yes, and why?" Irin seemed on edge.  I simply smiled. 



Yaereene stepped forward, hands folded with a kind of practiced grace that didn’t belong in a tannery. She held herself tall, spine straight, chin lifted — not arrogant, just… composed. Like someone used to being listened to.



“I’d like a battle dress.”



She said it calmly, as if this were the most natural place in the world to request such a thing. Not demanding. Not uncertain. Just… certain. Certain in a way that made me pause.



Irin didn’t share her sister’s stillness. She shifted her weight, arms crossing before she even realized she’d done it. Her eyes flicked between me and the door, reading the room for threats I didn’t see.



“Yes,” Irin added, sharper. “A battle dress. Why?”



The contrast between them was striking — Yaereene all quiet poise, Irin all bristle and readiness. I didn’t know who they were, not really. But I knew enough to recognize that people like this didn’t wander into my shop by accident.



And for reasons I couldn’t name yet, I already knew I’d say yes.



Before I could speak, Irin’s voice cut through the tannery’s stillness.



“Where even are we?”



She sounded irritated enough to chew through leather. Her arms were crossed, her stance defensive, her eyes darting like she expected the walls to shift. The bells on the door were still trembling behind her — she’d slammed it harder than she meant to.



But my attention didn’t land on her.



Curiosity tugged at me, quiet but insistent. Something about the way Yaereene held herself — calm in a place that wasn’t built for calm — made the air feel different. Like she’d brought a piece of the forest in with her.



Irin’s frustration prickled at the edges of the room, but I let it pass over me. Not out of disrespect — out of instinct. The kind that tells you which sound in the woods matters and which one is just wind.



Yaereene stepped forward, unbothered by her sister’s tone.



“We seek a craftsman,” she said gently. “And we were told you were skilled.”



Her voice was steady. Irin’s was a spark. And I stood between them, feeling the strange pull of both.



I wiped my hands on my apron, suddenly aware of the smell of tannin and smoke clinging to me.



“You’re in my workshop,” I said quietly. “And if it’s a battle dress you want… I can make one.”



Irin blinked, surprised I’d answered her sister instead of her.

Yaereene simply nodded, as if she’d expected nothing else.



And that was the moment — small, ordinary, forgettable to anyone else — when the path of my life bent toward theirs.



Where are we? Where can we get some food, some _sleep_? We’ve been on the run for seven days! I’m _done_ running!”



Irin’s voice cracked through the room like a snapped bowstring. Whatever admiration I’d been quietly nursing for her sister scattered. I turned toward the blue‑haired girl, really seeing her for the first time — the shaking in her hands, the exhaustion she was trying to hide behind anger.



“You’re in Grandfather Tree Village,” I said, keeping my tone steady. “My mother runs the inn near the Tree. She’ll give you food. A bed.”



I hesitated, the next words slipping out before I could stop them.



“And you’ve been running?”



That part hit me harder than I expected. Running. The way I had after my brother died. The way I’d run from the truth of it — from the fact that it was my parents’ hands that ended his life, not fate or war or anything clean.



Something in Irin’s posture — the tightness in her shoulders, the way she wouldn’t meet her sister’s eyes — felt too familiar.



I stepped a little closer, not enough to crowd her, just enough to show I was listening.



“What kind of grief do you carry?”



The question came out quieter than I meant. Not prying. Not demanding. Just… searching. The way you test the air before stepping into a clearing you’ve never walked before.



Yaereene’s breath caught, barely audible.  

Irin froze, anger flickering into something rawer, something she didn’t want anyone to see.



And for a moment, the tannery felt too small for all three of our ghosts.



## Irin

He really cared. He knew. Something in the way he asked — steady, not prying, not pitying — cracked straight through the wall I’d been holding up. All the resentment I’d been clinging to just… dissolved, leaving the grief underneath exposed and shaking.



“My mother — our mother — was killed by a really evil Lich,” Yaereene said.



The way she said our made something twist hard in my chest. She still claimed me. Still folded me into her family even when I felt like a stray she’d dragged into danger. Hearing her say it out loud made the pain sharper, not softer — like she’d pressed a thumb right into the bruise.



I stared at the floor because looking at either of them felt impossible. My throat burned. My hands wouldn’t stay still.



Yaereene’s voice stayed calm, but I could hear the fracture lines under it.

Ferris didn’t say anything — he just listened, really listened, like he understood what it meant to lose someone in a way that never stops echoing.



And for the first time in days, I felt the weight of everything we’d been running from settle on me fully. Not as fear. As grief. As truth.



As something I couldn’t outrun anyways. 



"I lost my brother last week. My parents' negligence led to his passing. They let him go on guard. He was shot by the other guards." Ferris volunteered his grief unprompted once Yaereene finished with her story. 

"He was the sweetest. He was two years younger. No one was uncomfortable around him."

I didn’t expect it to hit me like that.



One moment I was listening, and the next… it felt like someone had put a hand around my ribs and squeezed. Not hard. Just enough that I couldn’t take a full breath.



I heard the way he said brother and something in me recognized it before I could stop it. The kind of loss that comes from people who should have protected you choosing not to. The kind that leaves you standing in the wreckage wondering why the gentle ones are always the ones taken.



I tried to keep my face still, but I know my breath caught. I felt it. A small break in the rhythm.



I looked at Yae because I needed something steady for half a second. Then I looked back at him because he deserved someone who wasn’t looking away.



And the anger I’d been holding toward him… it just wasn’t there anymore. It dissolved. Not because I forgave him for anything, but because grief like that is familiar. It’s a language I understand without wanting to.



I stepped closer before I even thought about it. Not to touch him. Just to be near enough that he wouldn’t feel like he’d thrown his pain into an empty room.



When I spoke, I kept my voice low so it wouldn’t shake. I told him he shouldn’t have had to carry that alone. And I meant it. I could feel the truth of it in my throat.



I don’t know if he noticed, but for a moment… I wasn’t guarding anything. I just let myself stand there with him. In the shape of what he’d lost.





"I'm sorry. "



I didn’t think what I did next. I just moved.



When he said it — what happened to his brother — it hit me in a way I couldn’t push aside. It felt familiar in a way I didn’t want it to be. I didn’t try to hide that. I didn’t want to.



So I stepped in and hugged him.



Not careful. Not hesitant. Just… because he needed someone to hold onto, and I knew what that felt like.



When his shoulders dropped into me, I felt the weight of it. The kind you only let go of when someone else is close enough to take some of it for a moment.



I didn’t say anything. Anything I could’ve said would’ve made the moment smaller, and it wasn’t small. Not for him.



So I just held him. Long enough for his breath to stop shaking.



When Ferris finally pulled away, he did it like someone embarrassed to have been seen—like he’d remembered he was supposed to be the responsible one here.

“I’ll… I’ll get started on that. Any requests on color?”



“Red. Like this plant.”

Yaereene pulled it from her pocket, the same red bloom she’d taken from *our* mother’s side as she died. I watched Ferris’s face change the instant he saw it—recognition, shock, something like grief rising up too fast for him to hide.



He’d been searching the forest for something earlier. Maybe this was it. Maybe not. I didn’t know then. I’m not sure I know now.



“I… I… I didn’t— I’ll get right to it,” he stammered, and I felt the truth of it settle between us.



That was when I understood: he’d found his Feybloom the same way my sister had. Through loss. Through something torn away. Whatever bound those plants to people, it wasn’t random. And whatever bound Yaereene and Ferris… well, I still don’t have the words for that.



But that day, the grief between them was loud. I felt it humming off him, even if he didn’t realize I could.



“What brings you to the Grandfather Tree village?” Ferris asked as he worked, trying for casual. He wasn’t fooling me. I could feel the spark between him and my sister—bright, insistent, the same kind of spark that had shot out of my hands at the Lich earlier. They didn’t know it yet, but they were already in love.



“We’re fleeing,” Yaereene said.

“Fleeing what, exactly?”

“The evil Lich.”

Ferris snorted. “That guy sounds like a deadbeat. I pity your soul, Yaereene.”



She laughed—really laughed, from her belly, the way she hadn’t since the fire. Then her smile slipped.



“What I pity is meeting your mother,” she said. “She sounds… like she’s no fun to be around. She killed your brother?”



Ferris nodded. “My father and she.”



“Pfft.” Yaereene’s way of saying I’m sorry without making it a wound again.



“I’ll have this done in a few days,” Ferris said. “Come back then.”



But I knew he wouldn’t sleep. He’d be thinking about her. About the red bloom. About the grief they shared without speaking it aloud.



And for the first time since we stepped onto that strange fisherman’s boat, I felt something settle in me.



Peace.

Because my sister wasn’t alone anymore.

## Yaereene

We approached the inn Ferris claimed as his mother’s domain, a modest structure resting beneath the Grandfather Tree’s vast canopy. Its branches arched overhead like an old guardian, and the twin stairways spiraling around its trunk carried villagers upward as though the Tree itself had grown them for that purpose. The inn stood just left of the nearer staircase, close enough that the Tree’s presence pressed gently against its walls.



Crossing the threshold felt less like entering a building and more like stepping deeper into the Tree’s influence. Its magic stirred around us — not intrusive, merely aware. The floating lights drifted in slow arcs, their glow reminiscent of the radiance that had tried to devour us earlier, though softened now, as if the Tree had tempered it with intention. The air inside remained open and high, refusing to pretend it was separate from the world outside.



“Hello and—” Ferris’s mother began, then halted. Recognition flickered across her face, sharp and unguarded. In that instant, I understood her measure: if the Lich demanded my whereabouts, she would offer them without hesitation. Not out of cruelty. Out of fear, or habit, or the simple instinct to survive.



“—welcome to the Inn‑side Root,” she finished, smoothing her voice into something practiced. “Would you two—”



“Your son is paying for a single room tonight,” I said.



“Ferris? Why would the—” She caught herself, reshaping her disdain for her eldest into something more palatable. “Why would he pay for a… half‑elf like you?”



I had known, even before we crossed its borders, that the High Forest shared Feydor’s old cruelties. Its beauty was unmistakable—ancient boughs, drifting seelie lights, the quiet hum of faerie magic—but beneath it all lived the same hierarchy, the same quiet disdain for those not born of its favored blood. Ferris’s mother, half‑elf though she was, regarded Irin with a contempt so practiced it might as well have been tradition. The histories had not lied.



I took the keys from her hand without ceremony, meeting her gaze with a stillness sharp enough to cut. *Murderer*, my mind named her, though she had never touched a blade. Some crimes required no weapon.



I had known, long before we crossed its borders, that the High Forest shared Feydor’s old instincts. Beauty did not soften its prejudices. The canopy shimmered with elven grace, seelie light, and the drifting presence of faeries, yet beneath all that radiance lived the same quiet cruelty toward those not born of its favored blood. Ferris’s mother—half‑elf though she was—regarded Irin with a disdain so effortless it felt inherited. The histories had not exaggerated.



I took the keys from her hand without warmth, meeting her gaze with a stillness sharp enough to be its own accusation. *Murderer*, my mind named her, though she had never drawn a blade. Some harms were older, quieter, and far more deliberate.



Only then did I understand the true shape of my anger: it was not solely for her. It was for the half‑elf in the tannery, for the truth he had spoken, for the way I had believed him without hesitation. The realization struck like a sudden wingbeat beneath my ribs—strange, disorienting, almost painful. For a moment, I wanted to lift the small brass bell on her counter with my mind and send it crashing into the wall beside her. But the Lich’s shadow pressed cold fingers to the back of my thoughts, and I held still.



The room she gave us was lavish, almost absurdly so after a week of running. Its carved wood and soft light echoed the chambers Irin and I had left behind in Malynor—*home*, though the word felt fragile on my tongue.



“This is only temporary,” I murmured, perhaps to her, perhaps to myself.



Irin did not hear me. She threw herself onto the bed with the unguarded joy she somehow managed to keep alive through all of this.



“Sister, come sleep!” she called, bright and restless.



I found myself smiling despite everything.



Temporary or not, this was the safest we had been in days. Tonight, we could rest. Tonight, the Lich’s shadow could be kept at the door.



*We were at peace.*



## Ferris

I knocked on the door of Yaereene and Irin's room at around ten. The moon had just risen, giving the hallway a weird, half-ambient, half-unsettling feeling. Some may even call it _unsettling_. 

After a few seconds, Irin opened the door, with Yaereene following her, chastising her for opening the door for a stranger. 



She stilled, almost like a deer in headlights, when she saw who it was.



mother had asked me to bring the toiletries. Simple enough. A task with edges, something I couldn’t mess up.



I stood when it felt like I should, though my legs didn’t quite agree on how to do it naturally. The bundle felt heavier than it should’ve. Maybe because of what I’d tucked inside it. Maybe because I wasn’t going to explain that part.



“I brought what my mother set aside for you two,” I said, keeping my tone even, neutral. Nothing to draw attention.



I set the bundle down. Didn’t look at Yae. Didn’t trust myself to. If she saw anything in my face, she might ask, and I didn’t want her to feel like she owed me an answer.



The plant was buried beneath the soaps and cloths. The one she’d given me back in the tannery. I’d thought about leaving it where it was, pretending it was just another thing I’d been handed. But after hearing how she’d gotten here… it didn’t feel like mine to keep. Not anymore. Maybe not ever.



“It’s your toiletries,” I said, stepping back a little, hands folding behind me out of habit.



That was all I needed to say. The rest was hers to find, or not. I didn’t need her to know it came from me.



About earlier… I dumped—” Yae began, and the smile she gave me—small, apologetic, almost trembling—hit harder than anything she’d said before.



I lifted a hand before she could finish.

“I don’t need an apology,” I said, and it came out steadier than I felt. “I wasn’t uncomfortable. Honestly… sharing what I did was healing for me. As much as it was for you.”



She reached for the bundle in my hands. When her fingers brushed mine, something opened in my chest—quietly, shockingly. A feeling without a name, without a shape. Something I couldn’t track or measure or reason through. I didn’t understand it then. I only knew it felt vast, like staring across the ocean between Feyrun and Feydor and realizing it wasn’t just water—it was a depth I wasn’t prepared to fall into.



“Anyway… I should get on with my evening.”

I managed a smile that matched hers, or tried to, and stepped out before anything else inside me could give itself away.



The door clicked shut behind me.



I pressed my back to the patterned wall and breathed, slow and deliberate. This place had never felt like home after my parents sent my brother away. They’d offered me a room for life, but the walls were hollow now—echoes of what Brell and I used to be. We’d run these halls. We’d carved memories into them without meaning to. And then my parents took him, and with him they took every reason I had to love this place.



Losing those memories—having them ripped out of my hands by people who were supposed to protect us—felt like a kind of murder. Not of a person, but of a life. Of a childhood.



*Be kind to your kin*, the Code of Hunters teaches.

But how am I supposed to honor that when my own kin never offered me the same?



I pushed off the wall, steadied myself, and kept walking. Because that’s what I’ve always done. Because that’s what Brell would expect of me.



Perhaps that’s where the feeling in my chest came from, I told myself. I wanted that to be true—gods, I wanted it so badly—but something in me twisted wrong. This place had always unsettled me, even when Brell was alive. After he died, the air here felt heavier, like the walls remembered what happened and wouldn’t let me forget.



I stepped out into the morning light, trying to breathe past the tightness in my ribs, and that was when I saw it: a note pinned to the tannery door, a sprig of Feybloom tied neatly to the top.



My heart lurched. I didn’t need to open it to know what it was about. I didn’t need to read the name to know who had left it.



Yaereene.



And suddenly the feeling in my chest made a terrible kind of sense.



I read the note:

>Dearest Ferris,

>I did not understand what I was feeling until you brought the toiletries to my door at your mother’s hotel. It was a small gesture, but it unsettled me in a way I have not experienced in… a very long time. For whatever comfort it may offer, I do not fault you for your feelings toward her. Even in one night, her cruelty was plain. I heard how she spoke to her staff. And Irin—my sister, who stood beside me in your tannery—received a look I know far too well from Feydor. She was not welcome there. Your mother made that unmistakably clear.

>But that is not what has stayed with me.

>Your visit—brief, awkward, unexpectedly gentle—has remained in my thoughts far more than I intended. I keep trying to tell myself it was nothing more than courtesy, but the memory refuses to behave. It keeps returning, unbidden, warm in a way I do not have the right word for.

>By the time you read this, we will likely be gone. Perhaps that is for the best. It is easier to write these things than to speak them aloud, especially when I am not certain I could say them without… revealing more than I should. 

>So I leave you only this: thank you, for your kindness, and for something else I am not yet brave enough to name. 

>Yours,

Yaereene Inphyra



I didn’t make it to the door. The bell rang first—sharp, bright, too loud—and suddenly Yaereene and Irin were in my shop, and the world tilted.



She wasn’t wearing the dress I’d bled two nights into. She wore the green one, the one she’d arrived in, soft as new leaves and catching the light like it had been spun from dew. And the antlers—gods, the antlers—were crowned with that plant she’d brought me. Except now it had grown. Overnight. As if it had bloomed just to greet her.



My breath stuttered. Everything in me went hot, then cold, then nothing at all.



“It grew overnight,” she said, fingers brushing the leaves like she was afraid they’d vanish. “It just appeared there. I don’t know why.”



But I did.



The feeling I’d been trying to ignore since yesterday afternoon slammed into me with the force of a falling beam. Love. Not the soft, storybook kind. The kind that hits like a mistake you can’t undo. The kind that makes your knees feel unreliable.



I thought of my parents—how affection was something they rationed, how they sent Brell to his death without blinking, how every scrap of warmth in our childhood had been accidental. I’d spent years teaching myself not to want anything that could be taken away.



And then there was Yaereene.



The way she held her sister together while she was still breaking. The quiet steadiness. The beauty that wasn’t just divine, but human—earned, chosen, lived.



It was too much. All of it. Her. The plant. The light. The truth.



“I—” The word collapsed in my throat. My chest felt too small for my own heartbeat.



I couldn’t say it.



But I was in love.



And it terrified me.

## Irin

It was honestly hilarious watching the two of them just… freeze. Ferris stood there like someone had hit him with a board, and Yae wasn’t much better. He’d clearly read the note she left, and judging by the way his ears went pink, he felt exactly the same. Not that either of them would ever say it out loud without being pushed off a cliff first.



I’d practically forced Yae to write something before we left. She refused to confess outright—of course she did—so we settled on that half‑truth of a letter she could live with. I thought that would be the end of it.



Then her antlers bloomed.



It happened in a blink—pop—no warning, no glow, no dramatic music. One second they were bare, the next they were covered in dark red leaves like she’d stepped out of a painting. I didn’t need a sage or a priest to tell me what that meant. Unrequited love does that to Feybloom. And Yae’s was practically shouting.



Ferris finally managed to speak, though it came out strangled.

“Your… clothing.”

Yae gave him that tiny apologetic smile she always does when she’s embarrassed but pretending she isn’t. And I just stood there, biting the inside of my cheek so I wouldn’t laugh, because the two of them were hopeless. Hopeless and adorable and absolutely, spectacularly doomed.



“I have a… confession, Ferris…” she said, wearing that same little smile, all soft and pretty and painfully obvious. She hesitated—of course she did—so I nudged her. Literally. I was fourteen, charismatic, and deeply invested in getting these two idiots to admit what everyone else could already see.



“When I first met you…”



Ferris jumped in, voice cracking like he’d been holding the words in his mouth for hours. “You felt… funny?”



She laughed. “Yes, yes. But I didn’t realize what that was until my antlers bloomed. I did mention that I am a demi‑goddess, right?”



Ferris smiled—soft, reverent, like she’d just told him the sun rose for her personally. “No, but your beauty belied that fact.”



She laughed again, flustered but pleased. “I appreciate that.”



Then Ferris went to a cabinet and pulled out a bow—long, elegant, clearly crafted with care. “This is Brellich,” he said, voice gentler than I’d ever heard it. “It’s meant to protect you. And yes, before you ask, I named it after my brother.”



“That’s kind,” Yae murmured, already inspecting it like she was evaluating a sacred relic. She’d shown him her skill with a bow the day before, so of course she was impressed.



“I want to use it to protect you,” Ferris said, stepping closer. “To help you get your home back.”



“I’m honored,” she whispered.



And I smiled, because I knew exactly what Ferris was about to say next—long before he did. And I knew exactly how my sister would respond, too. They were predictable in the most endearing, frustrating way.



“In fact, Yaereene Inphyra, Princess of the Feydor Forest,” he said, voice trembling with sincerity, “I’d rather die than live without you. What says you?”



Yae froze. Then, in the most Yae way possible, she breathed, “I’m… honored. And I… I… feel the same."

I couldn’t hold it in anymore.



The second Yae whispered “I’m… honored,” in that tiny, breathy way she does when she’s overwhelmed, something in me just snapped. All the pride, all the smugness, all the “I told you so” I’d been swallowing for days came roaring up my throat like a geyser.



“Oh COME ON,” I blurted, throwing my hands in the air. “That’s it? That’s your big romantic answer? ‘I’m honored’? Yae, he just said he’d rather DIE than live without you!”



Ferris jumped like I’d thrown a bucket of cold water on him. Yae’s ears went pink. Good.



“And YOU,” I said, pointing at Ferris like an accusing prophet, “you’ve been mooning over her since the second she walked into your tannery, don’t even try to deny it. I saw your face. I saw your soul leave your body.”

“And BOTH of you,” I continued, because now that the dam had burst there was no stopping me, “have been acting like you’re the only ones who don’t know you’re in love. Do you have any idea how exhausting that is for the rest of us? I’m fourteen. I should not be the emotional adult here.”



Yae tried to shush me. Ferris looked like he wanted to sink into the floorboards.

Yae groaned into her hands. Ferris stared at her like she’d hung the moon.



I froze.



Not because I was embarrassed — I don’t do embarrassed — but because Yae looked at me with that soft, maddeningly serene expression and said,

“Irin… Ferris and I already knew.”



Already.

Knew.



My jaw dropped so hard it nearly hit the floor.

Ferris had the audacity to look sheepish. Yae bit her lip like she was trying not to laugh.

Well.

Finally, finally, *finally* we weren’t just the two of us trying to hold up the sky with our bare hands.



The sparks in my fingers wouldn’t calm down. They skittered and jumped like they were trying to tell the whole forest what had just happened. Fifteen minutes of terror and hope and grief and relief all tangled together, and my magic didn’t know which one to listen to, so it listened to all of them.



“Shall we go then, Yaereene and Irin? Get away from my mother, and towards that… that Lich?”



He said it like it was nothing. Like he hadn’t just changed everything.

Ferris did that. He didn’t even hesitate. And I think that’s when something in me grabbed onto him without asking permission.



He was our third brush with the fey — that’s what it really was— but back then it just felt like the world giving us one last chance. The fisherman disappeared. The seelie sent us on our way. I wasn’t thinking about destiny or anchors or any of that. I was thinking about him. About how he tugged Yae forward like she was a shield he didn’t want to admit he needed. Because for the first time since our mother died, since the world went dark and sharp and wrong…



We weren’t alone.

We weren’t just sisters clinging to each other in the ruins of what used to be— we were a family again.

And I felt it like lightning in my chest.
