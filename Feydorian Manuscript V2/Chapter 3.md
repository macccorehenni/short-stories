# Chapter 3— Fleeing
## Yaereene
I tried to revive my mother, but it was no use. My healing magic was weaker than hers. A thread so thin that it cracked at the slightest touch. Each spell dissolved into magic traces, no longer potent. I learned each incantation, each motion. 
But it all was for nothing. My mother's heart was not beating, and had not been for a hour. No mortal could survive having no pulse for an hour. Not even her. 
I wept, clutching her arm like it was the last warm thing in the world. The sound that tore out of me wasn’t a sob so much as an animal breaking open. My telekinesis surged before I could cage it. Pebbles ripped off the ground, not drifting — lurching upward, jagged and violent, circling me in a stuttering orbit that matched the tremor in my chest.

The air around me shivered. The earth flinched.
I didn’t care.
I couldn’t.

Grief had its teeth in me, and everything nearby rose as if trying to escape the pull.

As I cried, a plant sprouted from the ground— red, shining. I noticed it right away, and I paused. 
"Irin, look…" I smiled, despite all that had happened. 

My mother loved the Tree of Prophecy. She said its bark held the memory of every soul who had ever knelt beneath it. To me, it always looked ancient enough to remember the world before it learned to speak. A great, grizzled sentinel—older than the Forest, older than any story the elders bothered to write down.

My mother grew her magic around that tree the way roots find water—quietly, steadily, as if the Tree itself was teaching her how to breathe. She never forced anything. That wasn’t her way. She would stand beneath its branches with her hands open, letting the wind move through her fingers until the air thickened with that soft shimmer that meant her power was listening.

I didn’t understand it then, but she was shaping herself to the Tree’s rhythm. Letting its age slow her heartbeat. Letting its certainty settle her doubts. Every day she returned, and every day the magic clung to her a little more—like pollen, like memory, like the Tree had decided she was one of its own.

By the time I was old enough to notice, her power had the same texture as its bark: weathered, patient, impossible to shake. She didn’t just grow her magic there. She rooted it. And in some quiet way, I think the Tree rooted itself in her too.

This leaf looked like the vines that grew out of and around the Tree—those same living threads that shaped the cadence of my mother’s magic. Even as a child, I could feel the resemblance. The way it curled at the edges, the faint shimmer along its veins, the quiet sense that it was listening.

I called it Feybloom long before I understood anything about lineage or resonance. The name just… fit. It felt like something born from the same breath the Tree used to speak to her. And maybe that was naïve, but when you’re small and the world is full of mysteries that hum under your skin, you trust your instincts.

It seemed magical because it carried the same stillness she carried after standing beneath those branches. The same patience. The same quiet strength. I didn’t know the truth then, but I knew enough to recognize a piece of her when I held it in my hand. And I plucked it—carefully, like it might bruise if I breathed too hard—and slipped it into my scabbard. It felt wrong to leave it behind, like walking away from a whisper meant only for me.

The moment it touched the leather, something settled in my chest. Not a surge of power, not some grand revelation—just a quiet certainty, the kind my mother used to carry after standing beneath the Tree.

I didn’t know why I kept it. Maybe I wanted a piece of her magic close to me. Maybe I wanted proof that the Tree had noticed me too. Or maybe I simply wasn’t ready to let go of the first thing that ever felt like it belonged in my hands. 
“May your spirit be at ease, Mother.”  
I lowered myself until my forehead nearly touched the soil.  
She was the one who coaxed this plant into the world, who taught it to thrive where nothing else dared grow.
But the mantle had fallen to me now, and Feydor Forest waited.  
If I was to lead her, I would first have to reclaim her— branch by branch, breath by breath, from all that had taken hold.
But roots such as mine do not take hold with one tree. A single tree is only the beginning of a forest. Irin would learn to be by my side, and others would join the efforts we would make to repair our homeland. 
"Come, we continue, Irin."  
My voice steadied as my fingers brushed the scabbard of my grandfather’s blade.  
Something shifted beneath the leather—too deliberate to be chance.  

A note.  
Folded so thin it had become part of the weapon itself.  
It waited for me, patient as roots in winter, trusting I would one day look closely enough to see it.

My mother’s hand.  
Her script was the same calm curve she used when teaching me to breathe before I struck.

To her heir.  
To the root she believed would one day restore the Forest from its rot.

I unfolded it with the care one gives to a living thing, and read it. 

> My dearest half‑elf,
> 
> There is something I have held close for many years, waiting for the moment you would be ready to carry it.  
> If you are reading this, then either my time has ended sooner than I wished,  
> or you have reached your eighteenth year and stand on the threshold of your own choosing.  
> In either case, you deserve the truth spoken plainly.
> 
> King Calen is not your father.
> You were born of older blood than any crown can claim.  
> You are the daughter of Danché, the God of the Hunt.  
> On the night you came into this world, I stood beneath the Tree, and the vision found me with a clarity I could not mistake.
> He stepped from the shadow of its branches and spoke of you— not as a burden, not as a test,  but as a life entrusted to me with tenderness I had never seen in a god’s eyes.
> I carried you knowing exactly who you were, and loving you never once felt like courage.  
> It felt like breathing.
> Whatever path you choose from here, know this: nothing about your birth changes the truth of who raised you, or the depth of my pride in the person you have become.
> Stay strong, Yaereene. Your time will come. 

Tears came, but I didn’t slow. Irin followed at my back, and that meant I had no right to falter—not when she’d lost her whole bloodline and the woman who chose her. The red‑shirts were somewhere behind us, closing the distance. And as my mother always said, strength was not optional. So I steadied myself and kept going. A peppy glimmer rose on Irin’s face as she followed, and I held it in my mind like proof that I could not afford to fail her.
## Irin
Something in her face told me she was being brave on purpose, not because she felt it.
I copied her as best I could, though my eyes and hands kept spilling my nerves.
"Are you okay, Irin?" Yae smiled towards me. "We are going to get through this."
As if I didn’t already know that. I could tell, even back then, that my sister was the strong one—that if the thing we feared actually happened, she’d find a way to get us through.
The hills were beautiful. I’d only been outside Malynor twice, and never past the castle’s plateau, so the sight felt almost wrong to enjoy. But today? The castle town was burning. 
I really did *not* care about the beauty. I saw it, sure, but it meant nothing right then.
Survival was key. 
We walked, slowly, along a well-established path. It felt like the _ghosts_ of all who got hurt beacuse of my sister were angry with us. But her face? I could tell she was concentrating, thinking how we would survive. We needed to run, the sickening realization hit me. We couldn't stop running from those red-shirts until we killed them. But they trained and grew their immense and sick power from the Lich himself. And if Queen Imelda was any indication— we couldn't beat them. So we ran. 
After two painstaking, grief–fueled days on the run, we had reached the end of what Yaereene knew of Feydor, Fishermans Village. The village was quiet, a small one known for fishing and hunting. The vibe was generally friendly. No one seemed to realize who my sister was, or why she seemed to panic. We went to the village elder's house and asked for someone to take us in and protect us. All we got were skeptical refusals, despite Yaereene tearfully telling the story of what had unfolded in the capitol. They must have thought we were crazy, and we got no audience. Yaereene and I, rejected once again by a clueless fisherman, heard a shout of panic from the guard towers. They had caught up to us. 
## Yaereene
The man started his boat’s engine — a sharp, mechanical roar that made Irin exhale in relief, but sent a jolt through my chest.  
“The red‑shirts — I believe that is what you call them — will follow,” he warned.  
“Their knowledge of this world is greater than yours. They will use it against you. Take that to heart.”

Shouts rose on the dock.

The Lich’s voice cut through them, cold and unmistakable, and his red‑shirts seized a boat with magic crackling at their hands. They meant to pursue. Our engine lurched, coughing once before catching, and our unlikely savior — a deity still unnamed, even now as I stand among them — pulled us away from the pier.

We were headed for Feydorian Feyrun.

--- 
After fifteen minutes of travel, the fisherman turned toward me and Irin.  
“Beware of anyone claiming to love you without their heart.”  
He smiled, as though amused by something only he could see.  
“You will soon find the one in the unlikeliest place. But hearts not given space to grieve blacken. You may not like this, Queen Yaereene, but your stones have been cast.”

The title struck me like a pebble dropped into still water — small, but rippling outward.  
Queen.  
Stones cast.  
A path already chosen.

“While I think it best your father tells you more, or you find out yourself,” he continued, “I can tell you this much — your heart is in the right place, and you are part of a magical awakening.”

I didn’t know what awakening he meant.  
Nothing in my life felt awakened.  
If anything, everything felt half‑formed, waiting.

His gaze shifted to Irin.

“And Irin,” he said, smile widening, “your sister depends on you more than you know.”

Irin grumbled, “Tell me something I don’t know.”

He chuckled softly.  
“You, young Irin, are destined to more than even your sister, holding the line between two previously fragile lines.”

Irin blinked.  
“Two lines? What lines?”

The fisherman only tilted his head, as though listening to the river instead of her.

“You will find out.”

Irin’s frown deepened — irritation, confusion, that familiar spark of defiance.  
She hated riddles.  
She hated being told she would “find out.”

But for me… something eased.  
A tension I hadn’t realized I was carrying slipped from my shoulders.

If she had a purpose — even one wrapped in nonsense — then she was not drifting.  
Not lost.  
Not a weight I had to steady alone.

I was strangely comforted by her not knowing.  
By both of us not knowing.

The fisherman turned back to the water, as if the conversation were already over.  
The boat drifted on, carrying us toward a future neither of us could yet imagine — though apparently, he could.
I was okay with this. The past few days had been difficult for me in more ways than one.  
Not the kind of difficulty that bruises the skin — the kind that presses inward, reshaping the space behind the ribs.  
The kind that makes every word feel heavier than it should.

So if Irin had a purpose — even one wrapped in riddles and river-mist — then I could carry that.  
I could carry her not knowing.  
I could carry both of us not knowing.

It was almost a relief to let the fisherman’s nonsense settle without trying to solve it.  
For once, I didn’t need to understand.  
I only needed to keep moving forward, and keep Irin close.


--- 
A few days later, we arrived in the sleepy town of Archalen.  
Archalen was tucked against the west coast of Feyrûn, all salt‑soft air and weathered docks, the kind of place where time seemed to move at half its usual pace. Fishing nets hung like faded curtains between the posts, and gulls circled lazily overhead, unbothered by our arrival.

“I will take you up the river,” the fisherman declared, quiet resolve shining through his eyes.

There was something different about him here — as though the closer we came to the river’s mouth, the more solid he became. Less like a wanderer who had happened upon us, and more like someone returning to a place that recognized him.

Irin stretched her arms above her head with a groan.  
“Another boat,” she muttered. “Great.”

I didn’t blame her. The past few days had been a blur of travel, prophecy, and unanswered questions. My body felt the weight of it, but my mind felt heavier still.

The fisherman stepped onto the dock, offering a hand to steady me as I followed.  
His grip was warm, steady — too steady for someone who claimed to be just a fisherman.

“The river knows the way,” he said, as if that explained anything.

Irin shot me a look.  
I had no explanation to offer her.

Archalen’s townsfolk barely glanced our way as we passed. A few nodded politely, but most simply continued with their quiet routines — mending nets, hauling crates, sweeping sand from doorways. It was a place untouched by urgency.

For a moment, I envied them.

The fisherman led us toward a narrow vessel tied beneath a crooked pier.  
Not the boat we’d arrived in — this one was older, carved with symbols I didn’t recognize.

“I thought we were done with riddles,” Irin whispered.

“So did I,” I murmured back.

But the fisherman only smiled, that same knowing smile he’d worn on the open sea.

“Up the river,” he repeated, as though the river itself had asked for us.

And despite everything — the confusion, the exhaustion, the weight of words I still didn’t understand — I stepped into the boat first. 

---
A few days later, the fisherman dropped us off at Green City, the “gateway to the High Forest.”  
The air smelled different here — sharper, greener, touched by the breath of trees older than any kingdom I knew. The docks were crowded with traders and travelers, but somehow the city still felt hushed, as if the forest itself demanded quiet.

“We part ways here,” the fisherman said.

Irin hopped onto the pier first, stretching her legs with a groan. I followed, grateful to feel unmoving ground beneath my feet again. When I turned back to thank him, he was already untying the rope from the post.

“You’re not coming with us?” I asked.

He shook his head, that same unreadable calm in his eyes.  
“The river carried you where you needed to be. My part ends here.”

Before I could respond — before Irin could demand an explanation — he pushed off from the dock. The boat drifted backward, swallowed by the morning mist rolling in from the water.

One blink, and he was gone.

No farewell.  
No final riddle.  
Just absence, as sudden and complete as if he had never existed at all.

Irin scowled at the empty water.  
“Figures.”

I said nothing.  
Something about his disappearance felt intentional — not evasive, not cowardly, but… finished. As though he had delivered us to the exact place the world required, and any further presence would have been a lie.

Green City bustled around us, unaware that a man had vanished into mist.  
Unaware that two sisters stood on its docks with prophecy still clinging to their shoulders.

I took a breath, steadying myself.

Whatever waited in the High Forest, we would face it without him.
“Which way to the Forest?” Irin mused, turning in a slow circle as if the answer might be painted on the air itself.

I smiled — the kind of smile meant to steady myself more than her. Green City was larger than I’d expected, louder too, its edges softened by moss and the constant hush of wind through leaves. Every building seemed half‑claimed by vines, as though the forest were slowly pulling the city back into itself.

People moved with an easy confidence, unhurried but purposeful. Traders in green‑dyed cloaks bartered beneath canopies of woven branches. Children darted between stalls, their laughter echoing off stone that had long since surrendered to creeping ivy. Even the guards wore armor etched with leaf‑patterns, as if the city insisted on carrying the forest with it.

Irin wrinkled her nose at a passing cart piled high with herbs.  
“Smells like someone boiled a garden.”

“It is the gateway to the High Forest,” I reminded her, though the scent was sharp enough to make my eyes water too.

We made our way down the main thoroughfare, the cobblestones uneven beneath our boots. Lanterns hung from every post — not metal ones, but carved wooden frames holding softly glowing stones. The light they cast was warm, almost golden, and it made the whole city feel suspended between day and dusk.

“Do you think they have beds here?” Irin asked, eyeing a sign shaped like a leaf curling into itself.

“I hope so,” I murmured. My body ached from travel, and my mind felt stretched thin. A night indoors — any indoors — would be a mercy.

We searched for lodging, weaving through the crowd. Every inn we passed seemed to blend into the greenery, as if the buildings had grown rather than been built. Irin kept pointing out ones she liked, usually the ones with carved animals over the doorways.

I found myself watching the people instead — how they moved, how they spoke, how they seemed unbothered by the forest looming just beyond the city’s edge. As if living beside something ancient and unknowable was simply part of their daily rhythm.

I envied that ease.

“Come on,” Irin said, tugging my sleeve. “This one looks promising.”

I followed her toward a low, moss‑roofed inn with warm light spilling from its windows, grateful for the chance to rest — and for the brief illusion that we were just travelers, not two sisters carrying prophecy on our backs.

“Welcome! Are you int—” The innkeeper’s voice cut off the moment he saw us.

Not in our element.  
That was the polite way to put it.

His eyes flicked over our clothes — travel‑worn, salt‑stained, unmistakably foreign — then to Irin’s posture, which was already halfway between defensive and bored. I watched the shift in his expression: welcome, pause, recalculation. Green City was used to travelers, but not travelers like us.

“Oh,” he said, recovering with a stiff little nod. “You’re… not from around here.”

Irin raised an eyebrow. “What gave it away?”

The innkeeper blinked, unsure whether she was joking. I stepped forward before he could decide.

“We’re looking for lodging,” I said, keeping my voice steady, polite. “Just for the night.”

He relaxed a fraction, though his gaze still darted between us as if trying to place where we belonged. The people of Green City moved with the forest’s rhythm — grounded, unhurried. We must have looked like stones dropped into a still pond.

“Well,” he said at last, clearing his throat, “you’ve come to the right place. We’ve rooms open. Clean ones.”

Irin leaned sideways to whisper, not quietly enough, “Do they have beds?”

The innkeeper’s mouth twitched, unsure whether to laugh.  
I managed a small smile — the kind meant to smooth edges, not reveal anything.

Inside, the inn was warm and dim, lit by those soft golden stones the city favored. The scent of herbs and woodsmoke clung to the air. Irin’s eyes darted to the carved animals above the hearth — a fox, a bear, a bird with outstretched wings — while mine drifted to the people seated at the tables, their quiet conversations, their easy familiarity with one another.

We were strangers here.  
Obvious ones.

But for the first time in days, the strangeness didn’t feel threatening.  
Just… noticed.

The next day, as we left, the innkeeper seemed distracted.

He offered the same polite farewell as before, but his eyes kept sliding past us toward the street, as if expecting someone to appear. His hands fidgeted with the hem of his apron, twisting the fabric in small, nervous motions. Irin didn’t notice — she was already halfway down the steps, humming to herself, eager to see the city in daylight.

But I noticed.

People in Green City moved with an easy rhythm, unhurried and unbothered. The innkeeper’s tension cut against that rhythm like a blade.

He wasn’t looking at us.  
He was looking for someone else.

A cold understanding settled in my stomach.

*Red‑shirts.*

They had reached this far west faster than I expected. Or perhaps they had always been here, waiting for a description that finally matched two weary travelers with too many questions.

The innkeeper’s gaze flicked to me again — apologetic, guilty, afraid — before he quickly looked away.

He hadn’t turned us in yet.  
But he had been asked to.

I stepped onto the street, letting the door close behind me. Irin was already pointing at a vendor selling forestfruit, blissfully unaware.

I forced my voice steady.

“We shouldn’t linger.”

She blinked at me, confused. “Why not? We just got here.”

I didn’t answer. Not yet.

The innkeeper’s fear still clung to me like smoke.

We weren’t done running.  
The world wasn’t finished trying us.
